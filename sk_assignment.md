## Part 1 — Topic 7: Line and Polygon Clipping

Line and polygon clipping algorithms were first introduced formally in the computer graphics literature during the late 1960s and early 1970s, a period when raster display systems were becoming practical and researchers were grappling with the problem of efficiently rendering geometric content within the fixed boundaries of a physical screen. The earliest and most influential of these algorithms emerged from the practical demands of flight simulation and early interactive graphics systems, where the cost of drawing outside the screen boundary was not merely aesthetic but could cause serious problems ranging from excessive rendering time to hardware failures caused by attempting to address memory locations beyond the allocated display buffer.

The specific problem that clipping algorithms originally addressed was both a rendering optimization and a memory safety concern. In the earliest raster graphics systems, the display memory was a fixed-size array of pixel values corresponding exactly to the physical screen. Attempting to write a pixel at a coordinate outside the valid range of this array would either overwrite adjacent memory used for other purposes, causing program corruption or crashes, or require the hardware to handle an out-of-bounds address, which early systems often did not do gracefully. Beyond this hardware safety concern, drawing geometric primitives that extended far outside the visible screen region was computationally wasteful. A line that stretched from far off-screen on the left to far off-screen on the right might pass through only a small number of visible pixels, but a naive rasterization approach would still compute and attempt to draw all the pixels along the entire line's mathematical extent, including the vast majority that were invisible. Clipping algorithms solved this by computing the intersection of the geometric primitive with the screen boundary before rasterization began, reducing the set of pixels to be drawn to exactly those that were visible.

The Cohen-Sutherland line clipping algorithm, developed by Danny Cohen and Ivan Sutherland around 1967 and published in various forms in the early 1970s, was the first major solution to this problem and remains one of the most widely taught algorithms in computer graphics education to this day. The key insight of the Cohen-Sutherland algorithm is to classify the position of each endpoint of a line segment relative to the clipping rectangle by assigning it a four-bit region code. Each bit of the code indicates whether the endpoint lies outside one of the four boundaries of the clip rectangle: too far to the left, too far to the right, too far above, or too far below. An endpoint that lies within the clip rectangle receives a code of all zeros. An endpoint outside the clip rectangle on the left receives a code with its leftmost bit set, and so on.

Once both endpoints have been assigned region codes, the algorithm uses Boolean logic on the codes to handle three cases efficiently. If both endpoint codes are all zeros, the entire line segment lies within the clip rectangle and can be drawn immediately without any clipping. If the bitwise AND of the two endpoint codes is nonzero, both endpoints share at least one outside region, meaning the line segment lies entirely outside at least one boundary of the clip rectangle and can be rejected immediately without drawing anything. If neither of these quick tests applies, the line segment partially crosses the clip boundary and must be clipped by computing its intersection with one of the boundary edges, replacing the outside endpoint with the intersection point, and repeating the process. This combination of quick acceptance, quick rejection, and computed intersection made the Cohen-Sutherland algorithm dramatically faster than computing intersections for every line segment regardless of its position.

The Sutherland-Hodgman polygon clipping algorithm, published by Ivan Sutherland and Gary Hodgman in 1974, extended the clipping concept from line segments to arbitrary polygons. The fundamental challenge with polygon clipping is that the output of clipping a polygon against a rectangular boundary is itself a polygon, potentially with more or fewer vertices than the original, and with new vertices introduced at the boundary intersections. The Sutherland-Hodgman approach handles this by processing the polygon against each of the four clip boundaries in sequence, one boundary at a time. For each boundary, the algorithm traverses the polygon's edges one by one, applying a simple rule based on whether each vertex is inside or outside the current boundary and whether the edge crosses the boundary. The output of clipping against one boundary becomes the input to clipping against the next boundary, and after all four boundaries have been processed, the result is the correctly clipped polygon.

My own learning of clipping algorithms began with the Cohen-Sutherland method, and I remember the moment the region code idea became clear to me as a genuinely satisfying insight. Before I understood the region codes, I had thought of clipping as inevitably requiring intersection computations for every line segment. The revelation that you could assign a simple four-bit label to each endpoint and use bitwise operations to instantly identify which line segments were trivially inside or trivially outside the clip region, without any geometric computation at all, felt elegant in the way that only good algorithmic ideas feel. The early acceptance and early rejection tests handle the vast majority of line segments in a typical scene without any intersection computation, and intersection computation is needed only for the relatively small number of line segments that actually cross the boundary.

Working through the region code assignments on paper for a variety of endpoint positions helped me internalize the logic. I drew a clip rectangle on graph paper, labeled the nine regions it defined including the four corners, four edge regions, and the interior, and then verified that the bitwise AND test correctly identified trivially rejected line segments for each combination of endpoint regions. Tracing through several specific examples of partially intersecting line segments and following the algorithm's intersection computation step by step made the full procedure feel natural.

The Sutherland-Hodgman polygon clipping algorithm took more effort to internalize because the case analysis for each edge is more involved. For each edge of the polygon being clipped against a single clip boundary, there are four cases: both vertices inside, the starting vertex inside and the ending vertex outside, the starting vertex outside and the ending vertex inside, and both vertices outside. Each case produces a different set of output vertices. Working through these four cases carefully and convincing myself that the algorithm produced correct results for each one, including the cases involving newly introduced intersection vertices, was the key step in building confidence with the algorithm.

The way I visualize the Cohen-Sutherland algorithm in my mind draws on the image of a rectangular window in a wall, with line segments drawn on the surface of the wall that may or may not pass through the window. The region codes label the nine spatial regions defined by extending the four edges of the window into infinite lines, dividing the plane of the wall into the interior of the window and eight surrounding exterior regions. Each line segment has one endpoint in some region and another endpoint in some region. If both endpoints are in the interior, the entire line passes through the window. If both endpoints are in regions that share a common exterior boundary, the line is entirely outside. Otherwise, the line straddles the window boundary and requires trimming. I picture the trimming process as sliding one endpoint along the line toward the window until it hits the window edge, then replacing the original endpoint with this new edge-touching point and repeating the classification.

For the Sutherland-Hodgman algorithm, my mental image is of a sculptor progressively refining a shape. The polygon starts as its full, unclipped form. The first clip boundary acts like a straight cut, slicing away everything on one side and keeping what remains. The second clip boundary makes another straight cut on the surviving portion. After four cuts, one per boundary, the surviving shape is the correctly clipped polygon. New vertices appear wherever the cuts cross the original polygon edges, like the straight edges of a gem being cut from a rough stone.

Now, if I were to explain line and polygon clipping to someone without any engineering background, I would use the analogy of looking through a keyhole, which captures the essential idea very naturally. Imagine you are standing in a hallway and looking through a keyhole into the room beyond. The keyhole has a fixed shape, and you can only see what falls within the opening of the keyhole. Objects in the room that are positioned entirely within the area visible through the keyhole appear completely. Objects that are entirely off to the side, above, or below the keyhole's opening are completely invisible to you. Objects that partially overlap the keyhole's opening appear partially, with the portions that fall outside the keyhole simply not visible.

This is exactly what clipping does for a computer graphics rendering system. The clip rectangle is the keyhole. The geometric objects being rendered are the objects in the room. The clipping algorithm figures out, for each line or polygon, whether it falls entirely within the keyhole, entirely outside the keyhole, or partially overlapping the keyhole's edge. Entirely inside objects are drawn completely. Entirely outside objects are skipped entirely, saving all the work that would have been needed to draw them. Partially overlapping objects are trimmed to show only the portion that falls within the keyhole opening, and only that portion is drawn.

The region code system of the Cohen-Sutherland algorithm is like giving each object a label that says which direction it is from the keyhole. An object labeled as being to the left of the keyhole and above it cannot possibly be visible through the keyhole, because it is on the wrong side of both the left wall and the top wall of the keyhole simultaneously. Without even looking carefully at the object, you can immediately know it is invisible. Only when the labels are ambiguous, meaning the object might be partially visible, do you need to look more carefully and compute exactly where the object's edge intersects the keyhole boundary.

Clipping is one of those foundational computer graphics concepts whose importance becomes clear only when you consider what would happen without it. Every geometric primitive in a scene, whether it is visible or not, would need to be rasterized completely, consuming time proportional to its geometric extent rather than its visible extent. A single large triangle covering an area many times the screen size would consume enormous rendering resources even if only a tiny corner of it was actually visible. Clipping transforms this problem from one where rendering cost is proportional to scene complexity to one where rendering cost is proportional to visible complexity, which is precisely the property that makes real-time rendering of complex scenes computationally feasible.

## Part 2 — Topic 5: Medical MRI 3D Slice Viewer

A medical MRI three-dimensional slice viewer is a software system designed to take raw volumetric data produced by a magnetic resonance imaging scanner and present it to a physician in a form that allows detailed examination of internal anatomy. The system must accomplish several distinct tasks in sequence and in coordination: it must interpret and load the raw numerical data from the scanner's output files, it must map the physical dimensions of the scanned body region onto the pixel dimensions of the display device being used, it must provide tools for identifying and outlining specific anatomical structures within the image data, and it must allow the physician to navigate through the three-dimensional volume by scrolling through sequential cross-sectional slices. Each of these tasks draws deeply on specific computer graphics concepts, and the quality of the implementation in each area directly affects the physician's ability to make accurate diagnoses.

The first task, loading raw medical image files from the scanner, involves interpreting a volumetric data structure that is fundamentally a three-dimensional array of numerical values called voxels, which are the three-dimensional analog of pixels. Each voxel stores a numerical intensity value that corresponds to the magnetic resonance signal strength at that location in the patient's body, which in turn reflects the physical properties of the tissue at that point. Standard medical imaging formats such as DICOM organize this volumetric data along with metadata describing the physical dimensions of the scanned volume, the spacing between adjacent slices, the orientation of the scanning plane relative to the patient's body, and numerous other parameters needed to correctly interpret the numerical data as a representation of physical anatomy.

From a computer graphics perspective, loading this data is the process of constructing a three-dimensional raster image in memory, analogous to loading a two-dimensional raster image such as a JPEG or PNG but extended into a third dimension. Just as a two-dimensional raster image stores a color value at each pixel position defined by row and column indices, the volumetric MRI data stores an intensity value at each voxel position defined by three indices corresponding to position along three orthogonal axes. The graphics system must allocate a three-dimensional array in memory large enough to hold all voxels, read the intensity values from the file into this array, and interpret the metadata to understand the physical scale and orientation of the data. This physical scale information is essential for all subsequent processing, because the coordinate system in which the data is defined, measured in physical units such as millimeters, must be related correctly to the pixel coordinate system of the display.

The second task, mapping the physical dimensions of the scanned body region to the pixel dimensions of the doctor's tablet screen, is a direct application of window-to-viewport mapping, the same coordinate transformation concept that appears throughout computer graphics whenever data defined in one coordinate system must be displayed within another. The MRI volume is defined in physical world coordinates where distances are measured in millimeters. A cross-sectional slice through this volume is a two-dimensional image in these physical coordinates, perhaps representing an area of three hundred millimeters by two hundred and fifty millimeters corresponding to a cross-section through the chest or abdomen. The tablet screen has a fixed number of pixels, and a known physical size in millimeters based on the display specifications. The window-to-viewport mapping translates the physical coordinate extent of the selected slice into pixel coordinates on the screen.

This mapping must be performed carefully to preserve the diagnostic value of the image. If the mapping applies different scaling factors in the horizontal and vertical directions, circular cross-sections of blood vessels or roughly circular organ boundaries will appear as ellipses on screen, which could mislead a physician into overestimating or underestimating the actual dimensions of anatomical structures. The correct approach is to use uniform scaling, applying the same scale factor in both coordinate directions, and to center the scaled image within the available screen area with equal margins on the sides where the image does not fill the full extent of the screen. This ensures that the proportions of all anatomical structures are preserved exactly on screen.

The ability to zoom in on a specific region of the slice for detailed examination is also implemented through this same window-to-viewport mapping. When the physician pinches to zoom in on the tablet screen, the software narrows the window in world coordinates, selecting a smaller physical region of the slice, and the mapping stretches this smaller physical region to fill the same viewport area on screen. The result is that the visible region appears larger, revealing finer anatomical detail. The inverse operation, zooming out, widens the window to show a larger physical region within the same viewport. Panning across the slice corresponds to translating the window position in physical coordinates without changing its size, shifting which portion of the slice is displayed without changing the zoom level. All of these interactions are naturally expressed as manipulations of the window-to-viewport mapping parameters.

The third task, outlining specific organs or anatomical structures within the image data, draws on boundary detection and region filling algorithms that have deep roots in the computer graphics literature. In an MRI image, different tissues have different characteristic intensity values. Bone, soft tissue, fat, fluid, and pathological structures such as tumors each produce distinctive intensity ranges. Outlining a specific organ requires identifying the boundary between the tissue of interest and the surrounding tissues, which appears in the image as a transition between different intensity ranges.

The boundary detection problem in medical imaging is closely related to the region filling algorithms developed for general computer graphics applications. One approach, analogous to the flood fill algorithm used in paint programs, starts from a point known to be inside the organ of interest, selected either manually by the physician or automatically by a preprocessing step, and spreads outward through neighboring voxels as long as their intensity values remain within the expected range for the target tissue. When the spreading process encounters a voxel whose intensity falls outside the expected range, it treats that voxel as a boundary and stops spreading in that direction. The result is an identified set of voxels belonging to the organ, with the boundary being the set of organ voxels that are adjacent to non-organ voxels.

This three-dimensional flood fill in intensity space is computationally more demanding than its two-dimensional counterpart in a paint program, because the volume of data is much larger and the boundary conditions are defined by intensity thresholds rather than explicit boundary colors. The same stack overflow concern that affects recursive two-dimensional flood fill implementations applies even more severely in three dimensions, where the depth of recursion can reach into millions of levels for a large connected region. Medical imaging software invariably uses iterative implementations with explicit queue data structures rather than recursive implementations to avoid this problem.

Once the organ boundary has been identified in the volumetric data, rendering it as a visible outline on the two-dimensional slice view requires translating the three-dimensional boundary information back into two-dimensional image coordinates. For each displayed cross-sectional slice, the software identifies which boundary voxels intersect the plane of that slice, projects them into the slice's two-dimensional coordinate system, and renders them as a colored overlay on the intensity image. This projection is another coordinate transformation operation, mapping from the three-dimensional physical coordinate system of the MRI volume to the two-dimensional coordinate system of the slice plane, and then through the window-to-viewport mapping to the final pixel coordinates on the screen.

The accuracy of the organ outline depends critically on the precision and consistency of the coordinate transformations applied at each stage. Any error in the physical spacing metadata from the DICOM file, any inconsistency in how the slice plane is defined relative to the volume axes, or any rounding in the coordinate transformation calculations can cause the rendered outline to appear shifted or distorted relative to the underlying anatomy image. Medical software standards impose strict requirements on coordinate system handling precisely because inaccuracies in anatomical localization can have direct consequences for diagnosis and treatment planning.

The fourth task, scrolling through cross-sectional image matrices, is the primary interaction mode of the MRI slice viewer and brings together all of the preceding concepts in a continuous, real-time display pipeline. An MRI volume typically consists of dozens to hundreds of individual cross-sectional slices arranged along one axis, and the physician navigates through this stack of slices by dragging a scroll control or pressing navigation buttons. Each slice is a two-dimensional image in physical coordinates, and the viewer must rapidly load, map, and display successive slices as the physician scrolls.

The mathematical relationship between slices is defined by the sampling geometry of the MRI scanner. Slices are acquired at uniform physical intervals along the scanning axis, measured in millimeters, and each slice covers the same physical area in the plane perpendicular to the scanning axis. Scrolling from one slice to the next corresponds to advancing by one step along the scanning axis in the three-dimensional physical coordinate system. The image displayed for each slice is a two-dimensional cross-section of the three-dimensional intensity array, obtained by fixing the index along the scanning axis and retrieving all intensity values for that index value across the full extent of the other two axes.

The rendering of each slice image involves converting the raw integer intensity values stored in the voxel array into displayable pixel colors. MRI intensity values are typically stored as twelve or sixteen-bit integers spanning a range of several thousand discrete values, far exceeding the eight-bit range of a standard display channel. Displaying these values directly would compress most of the diagnostic information into a narrow range of barely distinguishable grays. The standard solution is to apply a window and level adjustment, which is itself an intensity-space version of the coordinate mapping concept. The physician specifies a window width, defining the range of intensity values to be displayed, and a window center or level, defining the midpoint of this range. Intensity values at the center of the window are mapped to middle gray on the display, values at the bottom of the window are mapped to black, values at the top are mapped to white, and values outside the window are clamped to black or white. This selective intensity mapping allows the physician to tune the display contrast to best reveal the anatomical structures of diagnostic interest, which may occupy only a small portion of the full intensity range present in the scan.

Scrolling smoothly through slices at a rate responsive to the physician's input requires efficient memory management and rendering. Loading each slice from disk as it is needed would introduce unacceptable delays. Medical imaging viewers typically preload a buffer of slices surrounding the currently displayed slice, so that scrolling in either direction can immediately display the next slice from memory rather than from disk. As the physician scrolls, new slices at the advancing edge of the buffer are loaded from disk in the background while the previously buffered slices at the receding edge are released, maintaining a moving window of preloaded slices centered on the current position. This buffer management strategy is an application of spatial locality principles to the sequential access pattern of slice navigation.

The combination of these four capabilities, loading and interpreting volumetric scanner data in physical coordinates, mapping those coordinates accurately to the pixel dimensions of the display device through window-to-viewport transformations, identifying organ boundaries through intensity-based region growing algorithms, and navigating the three-dimensional volume through efficient slice rendering with adjustable intensity windowing, forms a complete medical image viewing system grounded entirely in classical computer graphics concepts. The window-to-viewport mapping ensures that anatomical proportions are preserved accurately on screen, which is essential for measurement and diagnosis. The boundary detection algorithms allow physicians to identify and quantify specific anatomical structures and pathological findings. The intensity windowing applies the same coordinate scaling concept to the intensity domain rather than the spatial domain, allowing the display to be tuned to reveal the information most relevant to the clinical question at hand. Together these concepts demonstrate how the mathematical and algorithmic foundations of computer graphics, developed originally for engineering and entertainment applications, translate directly and powerfully into tools for medical diagnosis and patient care.
